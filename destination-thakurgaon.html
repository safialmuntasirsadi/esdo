Thakurgaon: Where History Meets the Harvest

A district shaped by ancient ponds, legendary mango trees, and a community that has preserved its agricultural heritage for generations — Thakurgaon is the quiet heart of northern Bangladesh's eco-tourism story.

📍 Northern Bangladesh, Rangpur Division 🗺️ Ramrai Dighi, Surjapuri, Sadar Upazila ⏱ Ideal visit: 2-3 days

Thakurgaon sits at the edge of Bangladesh's northern frontier — a district where the land is flat, the soil is rich, and the history runs deeper than most visitors expect. It is not a place that announces itself. You discover it slowly: through the shade of a centuries-old mango tree, the cool water of a Mughal-era pond, and the warmth of families who have farmed this land since before the borders were drawn.

For eco-tourism, Thakurgaon offers something rare: authenticity. There are no tourist traps, no staged performances, no souvenir shops selling imported goods. What exists here is a living landscape — agricultural, historical, and deeply connected to the rhythms of rural Bangladeshi life.

About Thakurgaon

Thakurgaon is one of the northernmost districts in Bangladesh, part of the Rangpur Division. It borders India's West Bengal to the west and is flanked by Panchagarh to the north, Dinajpur to the south, and Nilphamari to the east. The district is defined by its flat alluvial plains, fed by the Kulik, Tangon, and Punarbhaba rivers — waterways that have shaped both the agriculture and the culture of the region for centuries.

The name "Thakurgaon" derives from Thakur (a title of respect) and gaon (village), reflecting the area's historical role as a settlement of respected landowners and scholars. Historically, the district was part of the ancient kingdom of Pundravardhana and later came under Mughal and British administration before becoming part of East Pakistan in 1947 and Bangladesh in 1971.

Ramrai Dighi: A Pond That Holds History

If Thakurgaon has a landmark that defines it, it is Ramrai Dighi — a massive rectangular pond (dighi) that has survived centuries of political change, seasonal flooding, and the slow erosion of time. Located near the heart of the district, this pond is not just a body of water. It is a piece of living history.

Ramrai Dighi was excavated during the late Mughal period, likely in the 17th or 18th century, as part of a broader tradition of pond-building that served both practical and ceremonial purposes. In a region where water access determined survival, large dighis were symbols of power and community planning — they provided drinking water, irrigation, and fish stocks during dry seasons.

What makes Ramrai Dighi remarkable today is its condition. Despite its age, the pond retains its original rectangular structure, with embankments that have been maintained by successive generations. The surrounding area has been developed as a public park, making it a gathering place for families, a spot for evening walks, and — for the eco-tourist — a window into how water management shaped the social fabric of northern Bengal.

"In Bangladesh, every old pond tells a story. Ramrai Dighi tells the story of a community that understood water as life, power, and legacy — long before modern conservation existed."

— ESDO Community Heritage Documentation Project

For visitors, the experience of Ramrai Dighi is best understood through a guided walk around the embankment at sunset. Local guides — trained by ESDO's eco-tourism program — share stories about the pond's construction, the families who maintained it, and the role it played during the 1971 Liberation War as a shelter for displaced families.

The Surjapuri Mango Tree: A Living Monument

Not far from Ramrai Dighi stands a tree that locals treat with something approaching reverence. The Surjapuri mango tree is not simply old — it is legendary. Estimated to be several centuries old, this single tree has produced generations of the Surjapuri variety, a mango known for its distinctive flavor, thick skin, and ability to survive harsh monsoon conditions.

The Surjapuri mango (also called Shahibhag in some regions) is one of northern Bengal's most prized fruit varieties. Unlike the more commercially famous Langra or Himsagar, the Surjapuri is a local cultivar — grown, saved, and propagated by the families who have lived on this land for generations. It represents a form of agricultural biodiversity that is increasingly rare in Bangladesh's increasingly commercialized farming sector.

The mango orchards of Thakurgaon are not just farms — they are repositories of agricultural heritage, preserving cultivar varieties that have been passed down through generations of local farming families.

ESDO's eco-tourism initiative includes guided visits to the Surjapuri tree and the surrounding orchards during mango season (June to August). Visitors learn about traditional grafting techniques, the cultural significance of mango trees in Bengali rural life, and the challenges of preserving heritage fruit varieties in the face of commercial agriculture.

During peak season, local families host mango tasting sessions — an experience that goes far beyond anything available in a city market. The freshness, the variety, and the stories behind each cultivar transform a simple fruit into a cultural encounter.

How to Reach Thakurgaon

From Dhaka

  • By Bus: Direct bus services from Dhaka (Gabtoli/Mohakhali) to Thakurgaon take approximately 9-10 hours. Several operators run overnight services.
  • By Train: Take a train to Dinajpur station (the nearest major railhead), then travel by local transport to Thakurgaon (approximately 1 hour).
  • By Air: The nearest domestic airport is in Saidpur, Nilphamari. From Saidpur, Thakurgaon is approximately 1.5 hours by road.

From Thakurgaon Town

  • Ramrai Dighi: Walking distance from the town center.
  • Surjapuri area: Approximately 15-20 minutes by local transport from town.
  • ESDO Eco-Village: Arrange transport through ESDO's community tourism office in Thakurgaon town.

Traveling Responsibly in Thakurgaon

Thakurgaon's eco-tourism model is built on respect — for the land, the people, and the traditions that make this place what it is. Here's how to be a responsible visitor:

Do's

  • Ask before photographing people. In rural areas, always seek permission before taking photos of individuals, especially women and children.
  • Buy local produce. The mangoes, rice, and vegetables grown here are among the freshest you'll find. Purchase directly from farmers at local markets.
  • Participate, don't observe. When invited to join farming activities or family meals, engage actively. This is not performance — it is genuine hospitality.
  • Respect the pond. Ramrai Dighi is a living resource. Do not litter, and follow local guidelines about swimming and boating.

Don'ts

  • Don't remove anything from heritage sites. This includes soil, plants, or artifacts from around Ramrai Dighi and other historical locations.
  • Don't use single-use plastics. Carry reusable water bottles and bags. Thakurgaon's waste management systems are limited, and plastic pollution is a growing concern.
  • Don't visit during peak summer without preparation. April to June can be extremely hot. Plan accordingly with light clothing, hydration, and early morning activities.

ESDO's Work in Thakurgaon

ESDO has been active in Thakurgaon for decades, working on community development, women's empowerment, and environmental conservation. The eco-tourism initiative is an extension of this broader mission — using tourism as a tool for economic empowerment while preserving the district's natural and cultural heritage.

Specific programs include:

  • Homestay training: ESDO trains local families in hospitality standards, food safety, and guest management to ensure quality experiences for visitors.
  • Guide certification: Local youth are trained as heritage and nature guides, learning about the district's history, ecology, and storytelling techniques.
  • Revenue sharing model: A percentage of tourism revenue is reinvested into community projects — school improvements, health clinic supplies, and pond maintenance.
  • Heritage documentation: ESDO is actively documenting the oral histories, agricultural practices, and cultural traditions of Thakurgaon's communities, creating a living archive that visitors can access.

"The best way to understand Thakurgaon is not through a guidebook, but through the people who live here. Every family has a story, every tree has a name, every pond has a memory."

— ESDO Thakurgaon Community Tourism Office

Practical Information

🌤 Best Time to Visit

October to March offers pleasant weather (15-25°C). Mango season (June-August) is ideal for orchard visits but can be very hot.

💰 Estimated Budget

Homestay: ৳800-1,500/night. Meals: ৳300-500/day. Local transport: ৳200-500/day. A 2-day trip from Dhaka costs approximately ৳4,000-6,000.

🗣 Language

Bengali (Bangla) is the primary language. The local Thakurgaon dialect has distinct pronunciation. English is understood by ESDO guides and some homestay hosts.

📱 Connectivity

Mobile coverage is good in town areas (Grameenphone, Robi, Banglalink). 4G available in most locations. Power supply is generally stable.
